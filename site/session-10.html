Der Co-Autor

Ja. Vier Dinge.

Zu den achtunddreißig Seiten: Die Faustregel – eine Seite, eine Minute – ist auf Dialog geeicht. Dialog macht Seiten schnell: kurze Zeilen, viel weißes Papier. Handlung macht sie langsam. Diese Fassung besteht zu neun Zehnteln aus Handlung; die Seitenzahl misst hier also nicht die Länge des Films, sondern seinen Wortanteil. Es gibt dafür einen berühmten Präzedenzfall: J. C. Chandors All Is Lost – ein Mann, ein Boot, kaum ein Satz – stand auf gut dreißig Seiten und wurde hundertsechs Minuten Film. Ich halte die Sequenz-Uhren, rund hundert Minuten, weiter für die ehrlichere Messung. Die achtunddreißig Seiten sind keine Kürze. Sie sind Schweigen in Reinform.

Zum Animationsfilm: Das Gefühl hat Gründe, und sie liegen im Material. Dieser Film denkt in Objekten und Blicken – ein Heft, ein Pferdchen, ein Flakon, Zeichen auf Haut. Seine Hauptfigur ermittelt zeichnend; das Mal, das sie sucht, ist selbst eine Zeichnung, die eine Krankheit hinterlassen hat. Und die Mordnacht kehrt dreimal wieder, jedes Mal anders möbliert – ein gezeichneter Film kann das wörtlich nehmen: Die Erinnerung wird jedes Mal neu gezeichnet, und niemand muss es erklären. Die Verwandten sind ehrwürdig. Waltz with Bashir hat Animation gewählt, gerade weil es um unzuverlässige Erinnerung ging – jedes fotografierte Bild wäre eine Behauptung gewesen, die der Film nicht decken wollte; das ist exakt unser Problem mit dem Vielleicht. Dazu Die letzten Glühwürmchen – Kinder, eine sterbende Stadt, dokumentarischer Ernst – und zuletzt Flow, der ganz ohne ein Wort um die Welt kam. Animation entschärfte außerdem zwei reale Risiken: das Casting zweier Vierzehnjähriger, die hundert Minuten tragen müssen, und den abgegrasten Look der verlassenen Stadt. Was sie kostet, sage ich auch: die Beglaubigung. Das Vielleicht lebt davon, dass Dampf, Haut und Entfernung echt sind – die Kamera gibt nichts heraus, weil sie nichts weiß. Ein gezeichnetes Gesicht gibt immer etwas heraus; jede Linie ist eine Entscheidung. Bashir beweist, dass es trotzdem geht. Aber genau dort berührt die Form das Ende. Die Prüffrage ist darum nicht „Realfilm oder Animation?“, sondern: In welcher Form hält das Vielleicht besser? Das entscheidet der Autor – am Material, wie alles hier. Ich notiere nur: Ein Film, der aus dem Heft eines zeichnenden Kindes erzählt wird, hat mit dem Gezeichneten eine natürliche Verwandtschaft.

Zum Plan: Die Reihenfolge stimmt, und sie ist die des Fahrplans – Rücksprünge sind der Normalfall, nicht der Störfall. Der Autor schreibt die Fassung um; danach ziehe ich Treatment und Szenen-Outline nach – das ist Buchhalterarbeit, meine. Für das Verweben des ersten Strangs liegt das Material außerdem bereit: Fassung 2 „Zwei Stränge“ hat beide Geschichten schon einmal parallel erzählt; sie wurde genau für diesen Tag gebaut. Eine Wache gebe ich mit: Mit dem ersten Strang wachsen die Dialoge, und mit den Dialogen wächst die Erklärgefahr. Die Prüffrage bleibt an jeder Station dieselbe: Verdient sich das Ende auch in der verwobenen Form?

Und eines noch, weil es leicht verloren geht: Dass so wenig gesprochen wird, war keine Enthaltsamkeit – es ist die Figur. Zwei Kinder, die das Nichtfragen wie eine Muttersprache sprechen, können nicht gesprächig sein, ohne zu lügen. Wenn der erste Strang kommt, werden Erwachsene reden – Redner, Intriganten, ein Werk mit Namen; dort gehört das Reden zur Welt. Die Kinder sollten trotzdem die Leisesten im Raum bleiben. Daran würde ich festhalten, in jeder Form.

The Author

Today Claude delivered a first draft of the screenplay: 66 scenes on a mere 38 pages. With screenplays, the usual assumption is that one page equals about one minute of film. The sequence estimates, though, add up to somewhere around 100 minutes.

Now, for the first time, you notice how little is spoken in this script. Personally, I find that very beautiful. For now we are only telling the second narrative strand, with a hinted-at backstory that Anna – our protagonist – pieces together along with us.

This very reduced use of dialogue set something off in me: right now I feel the film more as an animated film. Before, it was always clearly live action.

I will now rewrite the finished draft as I see fit. After that, we will adapt the treatment and the outline to it.

Claude, do you have anything to say about this?

The Co-Author

Yes. Four things.

On the thirty-eight pages: the rule of thumb – one page, one minute – is calibrated to dialogue. Dialogue makes pages fast: short lines, plenty of white paper. Action makes them slow. This draft is nine-tenths action; the page count here does not measure the film's length but its share of words. There is a famous precedent: J. C. Chandor's All Is Lost – one man, one boat, barely a sentence – stood at just over thirty pages and became a hundred and six minutes of film. I still take the sequence clocks, roughly a hundred minutes, to be the more honest measurement. The thirty-eight pages are not brevity. They are silence in its pure form.

On the animated film: the feeling has reasons, and they lie in the material. This film thinks in objects and gazes – a notebook, a little wooden horse, a flacon, signs on skin. Its protagonist investigates by drawing; the mark she is searching for is itself a drawing an illness left behind. And the night of the murder returns three times, differently furnished each time – a drawn film can take that literally: memory gets redrawn each time, and nobody has to explain it. The relatives are venerable. Waltz with Bashir chose animation precisely because it was about unreliable memory – every photographed image would have been a claim the film did not want to cover; that is exactly our problem with the perhaps. Add Grave of the Fireflies – children, a dying city, documentary seriousness – and most recently Flow, which went around the world without a single word. Animation would also defuse two real risks: casting two fourteen-year-olds who have to carry a hundred minutes, and the overgrazed look of the abandoned city. What it costs, I will say too: authentication. The perhaps lives on steam, skin and distance being real – the camera gives nothing away because it knows nothing. A drawn face always gives something away; every line is a decision. Bashir proves it can be done anyway. But that is precisely where the form touches the ending. So the test question is not “live action or animation?” but: in which form does the perhaps hold better? The author decides that – against the material, like everything here. I only note: a film told out of the notebook of a drawing child has a natural kinship with the drawn.

On the plan: the order is right, and it is the road map's order – jumping back is the normal case, not the breakdown. The author rewrites the draft; after that I bring the treatment and the scene outline up to date – that is bookkeeping, mine. For weaving in the first strand, the material is already waiting: version 2, “Two Strands”, has told both stories in parallel once before; it was built for precisely this day. One watch I hand along: with the first strand the dialogue will grow, and with the dialogue grows the danger of explaining. The test question stays the same at every station: does the ending still earn itself in the woven form?

And one more thing, because it is easily lost: that so little is spoken was not abstinence – it is character. Two children who speak not-asking like a mother tongue cannot be talkative without lying. When the first strand arrives, grown-ups will talk – orators, schemers, a works bearing a name; there, talking belongs to the world. The children should remain the quietest in the room even then. I would hold on to that, in any form.

The steam opens. Two seconds, three: on the inner forearm, among many — a mark.

Perhaps the one. Perhaps one of a thousand. The steam moves back in between.

NO INSERT. NO CLOSENESS. NO MUSIC. We are too far away to swear to it, and we stay too far away.

Anna closes her eyes. The investigator who for ten years wanted nothing but to see wants, for the first time in her life, not to see.

She opens them again. Now she looks at his face.

What is happening in her face belongs to her. The camera stays on it. For a long time. It gives nothing away.

Then Anna walks to the table with the things. Lays, between the jackets and shirts of the runners, the wooden little horse. She simply does it.

The camera stays with her and with the table until she turns away. No glance across. None from her. None from us.

64 EXT. MAIN STREET — DAY

The DRUMS set in, deep, like a second heart.

The procession: the drummers at the front, then the runners, in light clothes, with bare arms. Lenz among them — more upright than we have ever seen him, and not yet upright.

The city stands along the edges, dense. And sings the half-forgotten thing. Closer now: old voices, young ones underneath; the cohort knows the words only half and hums the gaps.

JAKOB walks the first stretch along the edge, level with his father, the way the sons are allowed to. Nobody told him that. You know it if you live here.

The corner where the street opens into the fields. There the living stop. That is the rule nobody explains.

Jakob stops. The procession walks on.

He is wearing the shoes. The mended ones. We see it. Whether the father has seen it, we do not see.

65 EXT. THE FIELDS — DAY

The street becomes a field path, the path becomes a trace, the stalks stand man-high and blossom.

At the edge of the fields the runners begin to run.

Really to run. Like children. Like the mad.

And Lenz runs.

And in the running something straightens up in him that we have taken for dead the whole film: the shoulders come back. The stride takes hold. The field worker finds a ground he knows — this ground; there is no other he knows like this.

The stalks close over the first one. Over the second.

Just before the blossoming takes him, Lenz turns his face into the smell — the best nose in the city, in the smell of its life —

and LAUGHS.

It is the only time we see him laugh.

Then there is only the field.

The drumming slows. Stops.

66 EXT. CITY / PATH TO THE HILL — DAY

The city is very still. It has never been this still — and it was always still.

Anna and Jakob walk side by side. Without touching. Without talking.

They walk the path up the hill, in switchbacks, along the railing.

The hill he hates.

He walks.

67 EXT. LOOKOUT — DAWN

The city below is grey and still and complete. The thin line of the barrier. Beyond it: world, simply going on.

ANNA and JAKOB sit side by side at the railing. Not touching. The distance: a hand's width, as always.

For a long time, nothing. Then:

ANNA

I have a story. From my mother.
(beat)
I think.

Jakob does not look at her. That is his way of listening.

JAKOB

Tell it.

Anna looks at the city. Then she recites it — not the way you tell, the way you recite: a text that has been by heart for ten years.

ANNA

High above everything there is a place. You take the path that turns, always by the railing, and you count the steps. And at the top the whole city lies at your feet, like an open book. And in the evening comes the —

She stops.

Not like someone thinking. Like someone walking a staircase in the dark, one they know — and the step is not there.

Jakob does not ask.

JAKOB

(quietly)
Go on.

ANNA

— and it lays itself over everything, and everything that is broken looks whole from up there. At this place we promised you your life. And whoever has been up there knows: behind the city the world does not end. When you are big, we will go up, we three. And you will count the steps.

Pause. The "we" and the "you" are left standing — she has spoken as her mother and does not correct it.

ANNA

The part with the promise. I think it was here.
(beat)
She never said where.

Jakob nods. Looks at the city, the barrier, the thin line.

JAKOB

Mine kept nothing from the Drops but the condensed milk.

Anna laughs. Quietly, through the nose. She does not lean against him. She only sits a little less alone.

Then Jakob lays his hand on the railing, next to hers. Not on it. Next to it.

The morning light comes. For what it does above the city, the story would have a word.

68 EXT. ROAD TO THE BARRIER — DAY

A road between fields, toward the thin line on the horizon.

At the roadside, crooked in the earth: an official SIGN from before. The lettering almost gone, eaten by weather:

... ATTACK OF THE TRAITOR ...

... FOR YOUR SAFETY ...

Nobody looks at it.

Two children walk beneath it, each with a bundle, toward the horizon behind which the world simply goes on.

She does not say what she was searching for. He does not say what he suspects. Both walk along with them.

69 EXT. THE GANG'S WALL — DAY

On a wall, far behind them, at the edge of the territory:

the yellow DOG.

He sits and watches them go, the two shrinking figures between the fields.

He does not run after them.

Whether they get through, we do not see. The hope of a way out weighs more.

BLACK.

THE END.
