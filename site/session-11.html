Papier.

Sequenz 4 — Der Junge (~15 min)

AUSSEN. GANG-REVIER, WERKSTATT-ECKE — TAG

„Der Reparierer"

Anna kommt wieder. Diesmal mit einem Vorwand: ein rostiges Vorhängeschloss ohne Schlüssel, das sie vor Jakob auf das Brett legt.

Er nimmt es, ohne zu fragen, woher, wozu. Während er arbeitet, sehen wir seinen Platz im Stamm:

Ein Gang-Junge bringt einen Karren mit gebrochener Achse und lässt ihn wortlos da. Ein anderer holt die Falle des Anführers ab, gespannt, geölt, und nickt Jakob zu.

AUSSEN. GANG-REVIER, WERKSTATT-ECKE — TAG

„Der Tacho"

Ein Brett über zwei Fässern, darauf Werkzeug in einer Ordnung, die keiner Ordnung ähnelt und trotzdem eine ist. Jakob wartet. Vor ihm, in ein Tuch gewickelt, ein Gegenstand.

Anna kommt. Bleibt stehen. Der Abstand: zwei Schritte, wie immer.

JAKOB

Repariert.

Anna dreht das Ding um. Kein Riss, kein Rost. Und kein Fahrrad, nirgends — es gibt in dieser Stadt keine Fahrräder mehr.

ANNA

Er zeigt nichts an.

JAKOB

(ernst)
Null. Stimmt genau.

Anna sieht ihn lange an.

Dann stellt sie sich anders hin — das Kinn hoch, eine halbe Drehung, ein Taxieren aus den Augenwinkeln: der GANG-ANFÜHRER, auf den Millimeter. Tonlos. Exakt.

Jakobs Lachen kommt zu spät. Dann kommt es ganz — der ganze Körper, ein Lachen, das umfällt. Er geniert sich sofort, mitten drin, und lacht weiter.

Anna lacht nicht. Sie steckt den Tacho ein. Wie ein Werkzeug.

Sie geht. Jakob sieht ihr nach. Dann sortiert er Werkzeug, das sortiert ist.

AUSSEN./INNEN. GANG-REVIER → GASSEN → EINE TÜR — SPÄTER NACHMITTAG

„Das halbe Ding"

Der Junge, der nie von sich erzählt, hat eine Uhr im Körper.

Anna bleibt stehen. Vor der Schwelle.

INNEN. WERKSTATTWOHNUNG — SPÄTER NACHMITTAG

Jakob steht auf, stellt die Schüssel weg und geht hinaus.

INNEN. WERKSTATTWOHNUNG — NACHT

„Das Feldbett"

Das Fernglas, halb zusammengesetzt, auf dem Werktisch. Es ist spät geworden; das Petroleum brennt klein.

Niemand schickt Anna weg.

Sie sitzt in der Ecke bei der Tür, auf ihrem Platz — sie hat sich, ohne nachzudenken, den mit den zwei Fluchtwegen gesucht. Ruth schläft. Jakob räumt Werkzeug, das geräumt ist.

„Das Lachen"

RUTH

RUTH

Ist lange her.

„Die Sichtlinie"

Der Markt der Gangs, Alltag: Tauschen, Zählen, Zölle.

GANG-JUNGE

AUSSEN. DACH ÜBER DER GASSE — ABEND

„Zwei Geheimnisse, ein Frieden"

Zwei Kinder, ein flaches Dach, Reste von Essen zwischen ihnen.

Annas Heft liegt neben ihr — der Einband hat sich gelöst, das Vorsatzpapier hängt.

Jakob sieht es. Hält die Hand hin.

Anna sieht ihm zu. Genau. Die ganze Zeit.

JAKOB

Der Zwirn ist besser als das Heft.

ANNA

Ich weiß.

INNEN. — NACHT

„Die Nacht, zweites Mal"

Sequenz 5 — Die Lüge der Stadt (~15 min)

INNEN. FLURE DER STERBEZIMMER — TAG

„Wasser tragen"

Ein langer Flur in einem Haus, das einmal etwas anderes war. Türen, hinter denen gestorben wird. FREIWILLIGE tragen Wasser — Eimer, Kannen, Becher.

Anna trägt Wasser.

Sie hat ein System, und wir sehen es, ohne dass es einer erklärt: Sie nimmt die Zimmer, in denen gewaschen wird, die Flure, in denen Ärmel hochgekrempelt sind. Sie hält Becher, stützt Arme, sieht Handgelenke. Abends: zeichnen, durchstreichen.

INNEN. STERBEZIMMER — TAG

„Die Alte mit dem Takt, I"

ANNA

Was? Das, neh, wahrscheinlich nicht.

DIE ALTE

ANNA

Weiß nicht.

DIE ALTE

Terroristen bauen keine Kraftwerke, Kind. Konnte ja alles nicht schnell genug gehen.
(sie klopft den Takt auf die Bettkante)

DIE ALTE

AUSSEN. RUINE AM FLUSS — ABENDS

„JAH ERK"

Das Werk. Riesig, geborsten, von Pflanzen angefressen — die Ruine eines Stolzes.

AUSSEN. DIE FELDER — NACHT

„Die Ernte"

Hinter dem letzten Haus: die FELDER. Mannshohe Stauden, so weit das Mondlicht reicht, ein Blühen, das im Dunkeln grau aussieht — und ein Geruch, der betäubt; man sieht ihn den Gesichtern an.

ANFÜHRER

(halblaut, im Gehen)
Schneiden, nicht reißen.

Auf einer der Kisten, absurd sauber in all dem Schlamm: ein ETIKETT mit einem geschwungenen Zeichen: τ. Wir kennen es. Vom Plakat.

Anna schneidet, presst, trägt. Ihr Anteil: zwei Konserven. Eine davon wird sie verstecken.

AUSSEN. FELDRAND — NACHT

„Der Flakon"

Auf dem Rückweg, wo der Acker in Schlamm übergeht: Etwas glänzt.

Anna bückt sich. Ein FLAKON — das Glas vom Plakat, matt geworden, der schöne Verschluss angelaufen. Darin: ein Rest, ein paar Tropfen, dunkler als Wasser.

Sie sieht sich um. Niemand. Sie öffnet ihn.

Die Knie geben nach. Sie setzt sich in den Schlamm, mitten hinein. Die Augen laufen über — ohne dass irgendwas traurig wäre, das ist das Verstörende: Ihr Gesicht weiß von nichts, nur die Tränen laufen.

So findet Jakob sie. Er ist den anderen ein Stück hinterher, wie immer. Er bleibt stehen. Sieht sie an, im Schlamm, mit dem Fläschchen.

Er fragt nicht.

Er hockt sich neben sie, in Reichweite, nicht näher. Nach einer Weile, todernst:

JAKOB

Wenn du sie essen willst — die Blumen schmecken nach Seife.

Anna lacht. Und weint. Beides gleichzeitig, und sie weiß beides nicht zu erklären.

AUSSEN. DACH — ABEND

„Der erste Lauf"

Über die Stadt kommt ein Geräusch wie ein zweites Herz: TROMMELN. Nicht schnell. Tief.

Die HAUPTSTRASSE. Fackeln, ein Zug. Voran TROMMLER, dann — zwei Dutzend LÄUFER, alte Männer und Frauen, in leichten Sachen. Mit BLOSSEN ARMEN.

Anna, am Dachrand. Aus der Jacke: das Fernglas.

Zum ersten Mal durch heile Gläser — und das Erste, was sie scharf sieht, sind bloße Arme im Fackellicht.

Die Stadt steht an den Rändern der Straße, dicht, still — und singt. Etwas Halb-Vergessenes, mehr Summen als Lied — gezogen, schwer, in Wiederholungen, wie etwas, das beim Arbeiten entstanden sein muss. Es klingt nicht traurig. Es klingt auch nicht froh. Es klingt alt.

Am Stadtrand, wo die Straße in die Felder mündet, beginnen die Läufer zu rennen.

Wirklich zu rennen. Wie Kinder. Wie Verrückte.

Die Stauden schlucken einen nach dem anderen. Das Singen hört auf, nicht auf einmal — es franst aus. Dann ist nur noch das Blühen da, und das Trommeln, das langsamer wird wie ein sterbendes Herz. Dann hört auch das auf.

AUSSEN. RÜCKWEG ÜBER DIE DÄCHER — NACHT

„Nur ehrlicher"

Zwei Kinder auf einem First, hintereinander, langsam. Lange nichts als ihre Schritte.

Dann, ohne Anlass:

JAKOB

Mein Vater sagt, es ist wie Feiertag. Nur ehrlicher.

Er sagt es wie einen Witz, den man geerbt hat und nicht ganz versteht.

Anna antwortet nicht.

INNEN. STERBEZIMMER — TAG

„Die Alte, II: Frau"

Wieder die Alte. Sie ist weniger geworden.

Anna gießt Wasser nach. Die Alte, beiläufig, mitten aus einem Gedanken heraus:

DIE ALTE

Erzählen konnte der Jahn viel. Rosig, aber wie sagt man, mit wenig esprit. Seine Witze landeten stets im nirgendwo.
Seine Frau. Die war hübsch. Eine Lehrerin.
Die hat gelacht in seinen Reden. Mitten rein. Die einzige, die sie verstanden hat oder sich getraut hat.
Ach, eigentlich doch n guter Mann gewesen.

Anna gießt. Der Becher ist längst voll.

Anna stellt den Krug ab. Und fragt — eine Spur zu schnell:

ANNA

Habt Ihr mal einen Mann gesehen mit einem Mal am Unterarm? Hier.

Sie tippt auf die Innenseite ihres Unterarms.

Die Alte sieht sie an. Zum ersten Mal richtig.

DIE ALTE

Kind.
(Pause)
Hier sind alle gezeichnet.

Anna klappt das Heft zu. Steckt es weg. Nimmt den Krug und geht ohne sich umzudrehen.

Sequenz 6 — Der Aussichtsort (~12 min)

AUSSEN. WASSERTURM — TAG

„Die fehlende Stufe"

Anna klettert den Wasserturm — Routine, Griff um Griff, der höchste Punkt des Viertels. Oben, aus der Jacke: das Fernglas.

Sie schaut über die Stadt.

Plötzlich ein HÜGEL, am anderen Ende, hinter einem alten Depot. Ein Weg, der sich in Kehren hinaufdreht, ein Geländer als dünne Linie — und an einer Stelle, wo der Weg hinter den Schuppen verschwindet, setzt er aus. Eine Lücke im Bild.

Wie eine Treppe, der eine Stufe fehlt.

Anna sieht hin. Sieht weg. Sieht wieder hin.

AUSSEN. WEG ZUM AUSSICHTSORT — TAG

„Der Aufstieg"

Der Weg in Kehren, das Geländer: rostig, an manchen Stellen fort.

Anna geht voraus — und ihre Lippen zählen. Lautlos, aus Gewohnheit, aus etwas, das älter ist als Gewohnheit. Sie merkt es selbst nicht.

Jakob ist auch dabei, hintendran. Blass. Vorsichtig geht er hinter ihr her.

AUSSEN. AUSSICHTSORT — TAG

„Das Panorama"

Oben.

Der weite Blick: die ganze Stadt auf einmal. Die Ruine mit den abgestürzten Buchstaben. Die Felder, ein graues Meer bis zum Horizont. Die Absperrung: ein dünner Strich. Und dahinter, unfassbar —

Welt. Einfach weiter.

Jakob kommt an, seine Augen öffnen sich groß. Er versucht den Blick „einzuatmen“. Er stellt sich neben Anna. Wir sehen beide Gesichter.

Zum ersten Mal steht Anna an einem hohen Ort und ist nicht allein. Jakob aufgrund seiner Höhenangst sowieso.

Anna greift seine Hand.

AUSSEN. WEG VOM AUSSICHTSORT — TAG

„Weg wie weg"

Der Rückweg, in Kehren. Anna voraus, Jakob hintendran, eine Hand am Geländer, wo eins ist.

Unten, zwischen den Dächern, taucht die Absperrung immer wieder auf und verschwindet.

ANNA

Ist da mal einer durch?

JAKOB

(ohne stehen zu bleiben)
Einer von der Gang — sein Onkel hat einen gekannt, der's versucht hat.

ANNA

Und?

Jakob zuckt die Schultern. Es ist kein Schulterzucken. Es ist die ganze Antwort.

JAKOB

Weg.

ANNA

Weg wie durch? Oder weg wie weg?

JAKOB

Weg wie nie mehr gesehen.

Mehr gibt die Geschichte nicht her. Genau das ist die Geschichte: Es gibt niemanden, der einen kennt, der es weiß. Nur welche, die einen kannten, der weg ist.

Anna sieht zur Absperrung hinunter, bis die Dächer sie wieder schlucken.

INNEN. WERKSTATTWOHNUNG — MORGEN

„Ruths guter Tag, I: Die Kondensmilch"

Ruths guter Tag. Man erkennt ihn sofort: Sie sitzt halb, es ist Licht im Zimmer, und sie redet.

RUTH

Drei Jahre werfen die uns jetzt Kisten vom Himmel. Weißt du, was ich behalten hab, von dem ganzen Himmel?
(Pause)
Die Kondensmilch.

Jakob grinst über der Arbeit. Anna, am Tisch, lacht ihr Nasen-Lachen.

INNEN. WERKSTATTWOHNUNG — MITTAG

„Ruths guter Tag, II: Anton"

Das Licht ist gewandert. Ruth liegt wieder. Ihr Blick findet Jakob, und etwas darin ist verrutscht.

RUTH

Anton. Das Fenster.

Stille.

JAKOB

(ruhig, als wäre es sein Name)
Mach ich.

Er macht das Fenster zu. Er hält ihre Hand, bis sie schläft.

Anna sieht zu.

INNEN. WERKSTATTWOHNUNG — ABEND

„Ruths guter Tag, III: Jakob"

Das Licht ist fast weg. Ruth wacht auf. Sie sieht Jakob an — ganz klar, klarer als den ganzen Tag.

RUTH

Jakob.

Einmal. Richtig. Wie man ein Ding an seinen Platz stellt.

Jakob hält still, mitten in der Bewegung.

Mehr sagt sie nicht. Sie schläft wieder ein.

AUSSEN. HOF DER WERKSTATT — NACHT

„Lenz trinkt nichts"

Das Fenster der Werkstattwohnung: erleuchtet, klein. Drinnen sitzt Jakob am Bett.

Im Hof steht der Mann vom Markt.

LENZ (Ende 50, hager, lange Ärmel; Hände, die einmal etwas gekonnt haben). Jakobs Vater. Jetzt hat er einen Namen — für uns; die Stadt hat ihn uns noch nicht gesagt.

Neben ihm, auf dem Mauervorsprung: eine Flasche. Selbstgebrannt, voll, der Korken drin.

Lenz steht und trinkt nichts.

SPÄTER. Das Licht im Fenster ist klein geworden.

Drinnen steht Jakob auf. Er steht lange am Bett, ohne sich zu rühren.

Dann öffnet er das Fenster. Weit.

Lenz sieht das offene Fenster an. Er versteht es, wie man etwas versteht, worauf man die ganze Nacht gewartet hat.

Er setzt sich auf den Mauervorsprung, neben die volle Flasche. Er lässt sie zu.

So bleiben die beiden, jeder auf seiner Seite der Nacht: der Junge am offenen Fenster, der Mann bei der vollen Flasche. Bis es hell wird.

Ein ruhiger Tod.

AUSSEN. BRACHE / GRABSTELLE — TAG

„Die kleine Beerdigung"

Ein Stück Brache, das die Stadt zu so etwas gemacht hat — Reihen aus flachen Steinen, Holzmarken ohne Namen.

Särge gibt es nicht; Holz wärmt. Die Toten werden in Tücher genäht, die flachen Steine aus den Ruinen liegen gegen die Hunde. Namen stehen hier keine.

Ruths Tuch ist ordentlich genäht. Die Naht ist fein. Wir wissen, wessen Hand.

Wenige Menschen. Jakob. Lenz, zwei Schritte neben ihm — zwei Schritte, die keiner schließt. Der Anführer ist gekommen, mit zwei von der Gang; sie stehen hinten und halten die Mützen in den Händen, und man sieht: Das haben sie zu oft gemacht für ihr Alter.

Worte werden gesprochen, wenige. Wir hören sie nicht. Wir müssen nicht.

Anna steht am Rand, wo man steht, wenn man zu niemandem gehört.

AUSSEN. STADT — TAG

„Wie das Heim fiel"

Nach dem Grab. Sie gehen

Lange nichts.

Dann, mitten ins Gehen, ohne Anlauf:

ANNA

Im Heim hab ich Sachen gewusst. Wo der Zucker liegt. Wer nachts über den Hof geht.

Jakob sieht sie nicht an. Das ist seine Art zuzuhören.

ANNA

Einer wollte den Zucker. Ich hab's ihm gesagt. Für ein halbes Brot.

JAKOB

Und wenn sie ihn erwischt hätten?

ANNA

Ihn. Nicht mich.

Jakob nickt.

ANNA

Einer der Erzieher hat geklaut. Von den Rationen, seit Jahren. Alle wussten es.
(Pause)
Einmal, da hing ein Zettel von drüben. Mit der Zahl.

JAKOB

Und?

ANNA

Zu seinen Listen gelegt.

JAKOB

Und dann?

ANNA

Gewartet.

Jakob bleibt fast stehen. Dann lacht er — das Lachen, das umfällt, mitten auf der Straße, am Tag einer Beerdigung. Er geniert sich sofort und lacht weiter.

JAKOB

Du hast gewartet, bis es ein anderer vorliest.

ANNA

War sicherer.

JAKOB

(noch im Lachen)
Und der Erzieher?

ANNA

Dann war das Heim zu Ende.

Sie sagt es, wie man eine Rechnung zumacht. Jakob sieht sie von der Seite an — lange. Zum ersten Mal seit dem Grab ist sein Gesicht nicht mehr grau.

Er fragt nicht, warum sie ihm das erzählt hat. Es ist die längste Rede, die wir von ihr kennen.

INNEN. WERKSTATTWOHNUNG — TAG

„Der Souffleur"

Der Raum ist derselbe und ist es nicht: Das Bett, in dem Ruth lag, ist gemacht. Das Werkzeug an der Wand, säuberlich. Das Feldbett leer.

LENZ sitzt am Tisch. Vor ihm steht Wasser, das er nicht anrührt. Er ist nüchtern.

ANNA sitzt auf dem Hocker gegenüber — den sie sich, ohne nachzudenken, zur Tür hin gedreht hat. Jakob ist Essen holen.

LENZ

Die Felder. Früh um vier, im Sommer. Da war der Geruch am besten — vor der Sonne, wenn der Tau ihn noch festhält. Wir sind gerannt, um um vier draußen zu sein. Gerannt. Zur Arbeit.

ANNA dreht sich zu ihm.

Lenz sieht sie an. Lange. Fast ein Lächeln.

LENZ

Ich hatte einen Freund, der konnte keine Witze erzählen. Klügster Mensch, den ich je —
(bricht ab, setzt neu an)
Der hat dir eine Pflanze erklärt, dass du heulst, so schön war das. Aber ein Witz, drei Sätze — hat er nicht heil über die Straße gebracht. Er hat immer die Pointe zuerst gesagt. Immer. Aus Sorge, du könntest sie verpassen.

Anna hört zu, wie sie allen zuhört. Ihre Augen gehen einmal, beiläufig, zu seinen Ärmeln. Zugeknöpft.

ANNA

(beiläufig; das Beiläufig der Ermittlerin)
Wie hieß er?

Ein Atemzug. Lenz' Daumen reibt über den Tischrand.

LENZ

Mein Freund.

Anna registriert es.

Und Lenz redet weiter, als wäre nichts gefragt worden:

LENZ (CONT'D)

Ruth hielt ihn immer für ein bisschen steif, ihr gefiel seine Freundin besser. (schmunzelnd) Die hatten immer gut was zu Lachen....
Ich hab ihm alles vorgesagt. Jahrelang. Ich stand daneben wie beim Theater. Teilweise hab ihm seine eigenen Witze vorgesagt, halblaut, und die Leute haben gelacht, und er hat sich verbeugt.
(Pause)
Das war mein bester. Dass keiner gemerkt hat, wer ihn erzählt.

Anna sagt nichts. Aber sie steht nicht auf.

LENZ (CONT'D)

Den hier hat er nie heil über die Straße gebracht. Pass auf.

Er richtet sich auf. Kaum. Aber er richtet sich auf.

LENZ (CONT'D)

Fragt einer den Säufer: Warum trinkst du? Sagt der Säufer: Damit ich vergesse. Fragt der andere: Was denn?

Lenz lässt die Pause stehen. Genau so lang, wie sie muss.

LENZ (CONT'D)

Weiß nicht mehr.
(Beat)
Also wirkt's.

Anna lacht. Einmal, kurz, durch die Nase. Gegen ihren Willen.

Lenz sieht auf, als wäre Licht angegangen. Er geht ans Fenster, sieht nichts.

LENZ

(leise)
Noch einen?

Sequenz 7 — Das Kind der Lüge (~15 min)

INNEN. WERKSTATTWOHNUNG — TAG

„Der Bürgermeister"

Ein anderer Tag. Jakob ist unterwegs — ein Karren, eine Falle, irgendwas.

Als Anna klopft, stehen die zwei Becher schon auf dem Tisch.

Lenz ist schmaler geworden; es geht schnell jetzt. Am Hals, über dem Kragen: ein neues Mal.

Er sieht Annas Blick — den Blick, der immer auf Male fällt, sie kann es nicht abstellen. Er zeigt mit dem Daumen darauf, mit der Präzision eines Fachmanns:

LENZ

Der ist neu. Ich nenn ihn den Bürgermeister. Sitzt oben und tut nichts.

Sein Lachen ist trocken wie Reisig.

Anna sieht genauer hin. Unter dem Kragen, halb verdeckt: noch einer.

ANNA

(deadpan)
Unterm Kinn sitzt noch einer.

LENZ

Der Stellvertreter.

Anna lacht durch die Nase. Lenz sieht sie an mit diesem hungrigen Blick, und für einen Moment sind das hier zwei Menschen, denen es gut geht.

Die Uhr an ihm läuft. Wir sehen sie jetzt mit bloßem Auge.

AUSSEN. BRACHE / GRABSTELLE — TAG

„Anton"

Ruths Stein. Jakob hockt davor und richtet, was nicht verrutscht ist: den flachen Stein, die Holzmarke. Arbeit, die keine ist. Anna steht daneben.

Lange nichts.

ANNA

Wer ist Anton?

Jakob rückt den Stein einen Fingerbreit. Und wieder zurück.

JAKOB

Mein Bruder. Nie kennengelernt.
Ist damals nachts mit den anderen gestorben. Er war zwei. Mehr weiß ich auch nicht.

ANNA

Mmh. Schräg.

JAKOB

Mama meint es nicht böse.

Anna sieht ihn an.

Anna setzt sich auf den Mauerrest neben dem Grab. Und zahlt zurück:

ANNA

Ich weiß nicht, wer meine waren. Eltern.
(Pause)
Ich hab Stimmen. Flüstern, mehr nicht. Ein Wort, das laut war — einmal. Und eine Geschichte, die immer wieder kommt und irgendwie aufhört.

Jakob fragt nicht, wie sie weitergeht. Er fragt nicht mal, wovon sie handelt.

JAKOB

Erzählst du sie mir mal?

ANNA

(nach einer Zeit)
Vielleicht.

Es ist kein Nein. Beide wissen es.

Sie bleiben, bis die Sonne von der Brache ist.

INNEN. WERKSTATTWOHNUNG — TAG

„Die Stühle"

Ein anderer Tag, dasselbe Wasser, das er nicht trinkt. Die Nummern werden dunkler; sie rutschen näher an etwas heran.

LENZ

Ich hab mal einen Saal bestuhlt. Eine Nacht lang. Vierhundert Stühle. Für eine Versammlung, am Morgen —
(er richtet unsichtbare Stuhlreihen aus)
Stühle, Kind. Ich hab Stühle geschleppt für die Zukunft. Und derweil —

Abbruch. Die Hand wischt etwas vom Tisch, das nicht da ist.

Aber es kommt nichts mehr. Stattdessen, mitten aus dem Nichts:

LENZ

Wie alt bist du?

ANNA

Vierzehn.

Lenz nickt.

Dann steht er auf, räumt den Becher weg, der nicht weggeräumt werden muss.

INNEN. SPEICHER — NACHT

„Sätze nebeneinander"

Anna liegt wach, die Augen offen, das Dachfenster ein graues Rechteck.

Und die Sätze kommen. Als Stimmen, leise, aus dem Dunkel — in ihrem Kopf, mit uns als Zeugen:

DIE ALTE (OFF)

... eine Lehrerin. Die hat gelacht in seinen Reden ...

MUTTER (OFF)

... ein Ort, hoch über allem ...

LENZ (OFF)

Wie alt bist du?

Stille.

Anna setzt sich auf.

Sie sieht zum Dachfenster. Macht sich auf.

AUSSEN./INNEN. HEIM, ARCHIVFENSTER — NACHT

„Ich habe Tee"

Das Heim bei Nacht, von außen: dunkler geworden. Irgendwo brennt etwas Licht.

Anna klettert. Das Archivfenster — versteht sich — kennt sie von früher: der Riegel, der nicht ganz schließt.

Sie gleitet hinein —

Im Archivraum brennt Licht.

DER BLEISTIFT sitzt an dem kleinen Tisch zwischen den Regalen, als hätte er ein Jahrzehnt gewartet. Vor ihm: eine Kanne, zwei Tassen.

Zwei.

DER BLEISTIFT

Ich habe Tee.

Als wäre das eine Erklärung.

Anna steht am Fenster, halb drin, halb draußen. Er hat ihr Kommen nicht kommen sehen — er hat es ERHOFFT.

Sie kommt ganz herein.

Er gießt ein. Die Kanne zittert nicht.

DER BLEISTIFT

„Tee ist das einzige Getränk, das die Zeit anhält."
(Pause)
Stand in einem Buch. Das Buch ist verheizt worden. Der Satz nicht.

Anna trinkt. Es ist das Beste, was ihr seit Wochen angeboten wurde.

INNEN. ARCHIV — NACHT

„Das Pferdchen"

Regale, Ordner, die Reste einer Verwaltung. Anna steht vor dem Tisch.

ANNA

Meine Aufnahme. Da muss was sein. Ein Papier.

Der Bleistift sieht sie an. Dann steht er auf — und holt kein Papier.

Aus dem untersten Fach, hinter den Ordnern: ein kleines Bündel Stoff, verschnürt, zehn Jahre Staub in den Falten.

Er legt es vor sie hin und wickelt es aus, vorsichtig, mit seinen langen grauen Fingern.

Darin liegt ein HÖLZERNES PFERDCHEN.

Klein, handschmeichlerisch, die Mähne fast abgeliebt — ein Ding, das tausend Stunden in einer Kinderhand war.

DER BLEISTIFT

Das war bei dir, als du kamst.

Anna rührt sich nicht.

ANNA

Wer hat mich gebracht?

DER BLEISTIFT

Das weiß ich nicht. Nicht mehr, wahrscheinlich. Das Pferd hab ich damals versteckt, für den Fall. ...

Es passt in ihre Hand, als hätte die Hand zehn Jahre auf nichts anderes gewartet. Der Daumen findet eine Stelle an der Mähne von selbst.

Das Heft in ihrer Tasche wird schwer wie ein Mühlstein. Sie greift nicht danach.

Wir verstehen eine Sekunde vor ihr, und dann versteht sie.

INNEN. — NACHT

„Die Nacht, drittes Mal"

DIE NACHT, DRITTES MAL. Die fremdeste:

Das Zimmer ist fast leer. Die Möbel falsch, zu wenige, an den falschen Stellen. Wo das Fläschchen stand: nichts.

Und die Stimmen sind vertauscht — die Frauenstimme sagt, flüsternd, die Worte des Mannes; die Männerstimme ist ein Klang ohne Worte.

In der Hand des Kindes unter dem Tisch liegt, selbstverständlich, als wäre es immer da gewesen:

das Pferdchen.

(War es da? War es je da?)

Es KLOPFT. Der Riegel. Die Tür.

Und das laute Wort des Vaters — zehn Jahre lang nur Klang — ist zum ersten Mal fast Sprache: zwei Silben.

Zwei Silben, die „Lorenz" sein könnten. Oder alles andere.

Ob die Erinnerung das Wort endlich freigibt oder es gerade erst baut, ist nicht zu entscheiden. Wir haben eben gesehen, was sie mit einem Pferdchen kann.

Der Arm streckt sich ins Licht. Die Waffe. Das Mal. Überscharf — das Einzige, was immer gleich bleibt.

Und wir glauben ihm kein Bild mehr.

AUSSEN. DACH DES HEIMS — NACHT

„Die Überlagerung"

Anna sitzt auf dem First des Heims, wo sie als Kind saß. Unter ihr das dunkle Haus, vor ihr die dunklere Stadt.

Das Pferdchen in der Faust. Das Heft auf den Knien.

Annas Hand liegt auf der ersten Seite des Hefts.

Sie schlägt sie nicht auf.

INNEN. KLASSENRAUM, HEIM — NACHT

„Das vierte Gesicht"

Der Weg vom Dach führt durchs Haus. Ein Flur, eine offene Tür: der Klassenraum. Mondlicht auf leeren Bänken.

Anna will vorbei. Und geht hinein.

An der Wand hängt noch das Schaubild. Das Werk, stolz, im Anschnitt von unten. Davor, klein, die vier Menschen mit den Fahnen.

Anna hat dieses Bild tausend Stunden angesehen und nie gesehen.

Drei Gesichter sind ausgekratzt, das Grau speckig von Jahren. Das vierte hat niemand angerührt: eine junge Frau, lachend, ein Kind auf dem Arm.

Das Gesicht kennt Anna. Sie hat es zuletzt gesehen, als weniger davon übrig war.

Ruth.

Und neben ihr eine der Ausgekratzten: Das Gesicht ist weg, die Haltung ist geblieben. Sie steht ein wenig gedreht, und eine Hand liegt auf dem Kleid, tief, wie Hände liegen, wenn sie etwas bewachen.

Anna steht lange vor dem Bild und streicht der Frau über den Bauch.

AUSSEN. DESTILLERIE, MAUER — TAG

„Die Kreideliste"

Die alte Destillerie: ein langer Backsteinbau, tote Schornsteine, der Geruch von damals längst aus den Wänden.

An der Hofmauer: NAMEN. Mit Kreide geschrieben, untereinander, manche verwittert, manche frisch. Eine Liste, die niemand verwaltet und alle respektieren.

Wer hier steht, wird gewaschen und läuft.

JAKOB steht vor der Mauer. Allein.

Sein Blick geht die Namen durch — und bleibt hängen.

Da steht, in ordentlicher, altmodischer Handschrift, mit vollem Namen, Vor- und Zuname, frisch:

LORENZ KESSLER

Jakob steht sehr still.

AUSSEN. VOR DER WERKSTATT — TAG

„Die Bitte"

Anna kommt — und Jakob steht schon da, vor der Tür, als hätte er nicht hineingekonnt.

JAKOB

Er hat sich eingetragen. Ordentlich. Mit vollem Namen.

Anna versteht sofort, was gemeint ist. Die ganze Stadt verstünde es.

ANNA

(automatisch)
Wie heißt er?

JAKOB

Lorenz.

Stille.

In Anna klingt etwas an — zwei Silben, tief unter zehn Jahren. Oder sie baut es in diesem Moment. Es ist nicht zu entscheiden, nicht für sie, nicht für uns.

Ihr Gesicht tut nichts. Das ist Arbeit, und man sieht die Arbeit.

Dann, zum ersten Mal, seit wir ihn kennen, will Jakob etwas:

JAKOB

Du bist schnell. Dir fällt was ein.
(Pause)
Hilf mir, ihn da runterzukriegen.

Anna — die ihm nie eine Bitte abgeschlagen hat, weil er nie eine hatte — sagt nichts.

Eine Sekunde. Zwei. Eine Sekunde zu lang.

Jakob sieht sie an. Sieht irgendwas, das er nicht benennen kann.

JAKOB

Du schaust ihn an wie deine Erwachsenen.
(leiser)

ANNA

Runterkriegen. Wie denn. Sag mir, wie.

JAKOB

Dir fällt sonst alles ein. Beim Zucker. Bei den Listen. Bei deinem Hund ist dir auch was eingefallen —

ANNA

Der Hund war ein Zoll.

JAKOB

Der Hund war deiner. Du hast nicht mal gewartet, bis sie ihn festhalten. Du hast genickt und bist gegangen.

ANNA

Und du bist mir auf jedes Dach nachgeklettert, grau wie Asche, und hast keinen Ton gesagt. Versteckt hattest du deinen Vater. Ich sehe alles.
(Pause)
Sieh du ihn dir mal an. Er hat nicht mehr lange — von der Wand oder nicht.
(ruhig; das Ruhige ist das Schlimmste)

JAKOB

(der leiseste Satz des Streits)
Ja. Du siehst alles. Und sagst nichts. Du sitzt bei ihm, du hörst ihm zu, seit Wochen — und wenn ich einmal was brauch, rechnest du.

ANNA

Ich rechne nicht —

JAKOB

Ich hab zugesehen. Eine Sekunde. Du hast gerechnet wie am Zoll.

JAKOB

Leute, die immer nur nehmen, kenn ich. Die ganze Stadt nimmt. Ich dachte —

Er bricht ab. Er sagt nicht, was er dachte.

Jakob geht. Anna bleibt stehen und sieht ihm nach.

AUSSEN. VOR DER WERKSTATT — NACHT

The Co-Author

Gladly. The spyglass first, because it shows how this workshop works.

At the beginning stood not an idea but a mistake: Anna was observing things no human eye could see at that distance. The author did not paper over the mistake — he gave it a prop, and the prop immediately took on more work than it had been assigned. The spyglass is blind when we first meet it: it lies at the bottom of Anna's bundle, a dent in the tube, whoever looks through it sees milk — and it gets the third hiding place, the best one. It becomes the pretext on which she meets Jakob again: at last a real defect. It is the first whole glass she sees the city through — and the first thing she sees sharp is the bare arms of the runners. It finds the hill with the missing step. After the fight, Anna sets it on Jakob's threshold — her best thing, not a return, an apology in their common language. And at the end it hangs on a new strap around Jakob's neck. A logic hole has become the biography of a prop — one that tells, in passing, what happens between two people who cannot talk about themselves. The workshop sentence for it: the best prop is one that takes over work the dialogue would otherwise have to do.

On the location problem: the finding is right, and I will give the number. In sequences six and seven, six scenes play in the same workshop flat. There is an honest reason — the flat is the only place where this film has anything like a home, and visits are the form of that relationship. But the film loses the city over it, and the city is half the story. The walking scene the author means has been in the script since this week: after the funeral, Anna and Jakob walk through the streets because standing still would be worse, and Anna tells about herself for the first time. Scenes like this do three things at once: they give the film the city back, they make time jumps credible, and they give two children who would never look at each other across a table the casualness in which talking becomes possible. It is easier to talk walking side by side than sitting face to face — that is not screenwriting wisdom, everyone knows it from walks. The film can easily take one more of these. Just one watch to hand along: every walking scene needs a load of its own — something that changes inside it. The moment the walking merely puts distance between two interiors, it is filler, and the audience senses it before we do.

On the development the author notices: it is the real yield of these weeks. The storyline from twenty years ago knew everything — who shot, why, what happened. The draft that exists now knows only what Anna knows, and does not even trust that: the night of the murder returns three times, furnished differently each time; a little wooden horse suddenly lies in a memory it cannot ever have lain in; and whether the loud word at the door was ever a name the film does not give away. The question "What happened?" has become the question "What can she know — and what is she building?". That is not a departure from the old storyline. It is its translation out of the file into a gaze.

On the worry about scenes for later: it is unfounded, for exactly the reason the author himself names. The single-strand draft is the workbench. What falls out when the two storylines are woven together is not lost — small scenes with a clear load are precisely the material you need between time jumps. And version 2, which has already told both strands in parallel once, lies ready for that day.

And now the chronicle, as requested — what actually changed in the last rounds, so you can watch us work. The rhythm is always the same: the author rearranges, cuts, rewrites and leaves comments in the screenplay file; I present findings and text proposals; the author decides; then I bring the workshop documents up to date.

First, the author's rearrangements. The home sequence has been resorted: the slip of paper with the number — the proof against the thieving houseparent — now surfaces earlier; Anna's mockery number, in which she imitates the thief for the little ones, comes only after his public exposure; and the revolt breaks loose the morning after. Three events have become a fuse. At the lookout, Anna now takes Jakob's hand — the first touch of the film, and it comes from her; that Jakob, at the very end, lays his hand only next to hers, not on it, has been an echo ever since. And at the last run, Anna arrives at the corner where the living stop.

Second, the comments. On the scene with the mended sole stood the question: when did that actually happen — should it stay unclear? The finding: the timeline is already in the material. Until the toll, the dog lay guard at Anna's door; the gang takes him as the price; and in the first night without a guard, Jakob gets through. The price she paid opens the gap through which, for the first time, something good gets through to her. The decision: it stays untold — only one look was added, to the empty spot by the door where the dog used to lie.

On one small scene stood the best question of this round: why does this scene exist at all? The check showed: it consisted almost entirely of sentences no camera can film — "She does not recognize him," "Nobody in the room knows how new this is." Pretty on paper, invisible on screen. The scene is cut; its single filmable piece of information is now carried by a prop: when Anna knocks, the two cups are already standing on the table. The author's question has become a new working principle, the fourteenth: paper does not count — only what the camera sees is told.

Third, the hole the author found. In the big fight, Jakob throws things at Anna: the sugar, the lists. Except — Jakob could not have known any of it; nobody ever told him. This error had been in the text since the very first draft and slipped through every check, including mine. The answer is the new walking scene: after the funeral, Anna tells, unasked, matter-of-fact as a work report, how the home fell. Jakob laughs at the point where the thief falls, and puts her method into one admiring sentence: "You waited for someone else to read it out." It is the same sentence he gives back to her in the fight as an accusation: "You see everything. And say nothing." A scene that closes a logic hole and, in passing, hands the fight its ammunition — repairs like that are the best kind.

And so nobody thinks all we do here is nod: one comment is open. On the night scene in which Anna's suspicion assembles itself, the author's note still stands — tell it more elegantly. My proposal for it was presented and not accepted; the author is making his own. That too is this workshop: the note stays in the file until the better version is found.

He does not raise his voice. He raises nothing at all. He only stands so that she knows he has seen her.

Beyond the gate: the city. Grey, staggered.

She walks in.

Sequence 3 — The City (~12 min)

EXT. STREETS — DAY

"The Rules"

The city by day: emptier than a city is allowed to be, and not empty enough to be dead.

Anna walks — and learns.

At a corner: a CHALK X, hip-high, retraced many times. Ahead of Anna a shrouded FIGURE with a bucket turns off without looking at the X. Anna turns off too, following.

A well with a bucket on it, upside down. The figure does not draw water here; it walks two streets further to a well without a bucket. Anna memorizes both.

Doors with signs hanging at them — on the post, at hand height: a strip of cloth, a piece of tin, once a child's shoe.

In front of these doors you walk on the other side of the street. Anna walks on the other side of the street.

Nobody explains anything. Anna asks nothing. We learn the way she learns: by watching.

EXT. BLANK WALL — DAY

"Can You Still Smell It?"

A windowless wall, six stories high. On it, huge and faded: an ADVERTISING POSTER.

A glass flacon, perfect of form. A flower, painted like a promise. And a sign, curved like a signature — the Greek letter: τ. Beneath it, in elegant lettering: TAU.

Across it, in tar, in letters that dripped as they were written:

CAN YOU STILL SMELL IT?

Anna walks past without reading.

EXT. WASTELAND — DAY

"The Dog"

Anna, motionless behind a remnant of wall, a wire snare in her hand, her eyes on a PIGEON pecking among the rubble.

She throws. Too late —

Something yellow is faster. A DOG, thin, yellow, takes the pigeon mid-leap, lands, stands.

He does not eat. He looks at Anna, the pigeon in his jaws.

Anna looks at the dog. And you can watch her calculate — it is the same face as with the sugar, the thief, the lists: meat for two days. Or that — what he just did — every day.

She crouches down. Waits. The dog comes, in a wide arc, and does not put the pigeon down — but he lets Anna lay her hand on it.

She shares. Half, half. He eats his at once. She wraps hers up.

The dog stays.

EXT. ALCOVE BETWEEN TWO HOUSES — NIGHT

"Without Calculation"

Anna sleeps sitting up, her back against the wall. The dog lies behind her, a warm half-circle around her back.

Half asleep, the dog lays his head on her leg.

Anna's hand is in his fur before she notices. For a moment her face is fourteen years old and nothing else — no bookkeeping, no calculation.

She notices. Startles. Pulls her hand away.

The dog lifts his head, looks at her.

She puts her hand back.

EXT./INT. ATTIC ABOVE A DEAD LANE — DAY

"Sleeping High"

Anna climbs a facade nobody considers climbable anymore — drainpipe, ledge, window.

The attic: empty, dusty, high. Two ways out — the hatch to the lane, the skylight to the ridge. Anna checks both. Only then does she set down her bundle.

The notebook gets a hiding place: under a floorboard she lifts with the tool of a practiced hand. The half ration gets another: behind the chimney flue, two rooms away.

Always half. Always somewhere else.

From the bundle, at the very bottom: a SPYGLASS. Small, brass, a dent in the tube — blind. Whoever looks through it sees milk. Deep inside the housing, something loose rattles.

Anna holds it to her eye.

Then she wraps it up and hides it — not with the ration, not with the notebook. A third hiding place. The best one.

Last, she lies down at the skylight and looks down into the lane. Whoever sleeps high hears the city from above.

The dog has found the staircase she did not take. He lies down at the door as if that were his post.

INT. WASH HOUSE — DAY

"The Archive of Not-Finding"

Steam, tubs, washboards. The GROWN-UPS OF THE CITY, for the first time many at once: shrouded, long-sleeved, grey — the MARKED. Washing, some roll up their sleeves. Only here.

Anna carries water, wrings, makes herself useful — and looks.

Not at faces. At wrists. Forearms. The strip of skin between glove and sleeve.

EXT./INT. ATTIC ABOVE A DEAD LANE — NIGHT

Anna sits before a small fire that gives light; in front of her lies the dog. On a spit, a skinned rat.

Anna draws marks on paper from memory and crosses them out, one after another.

She lays the notebook aside.

Anna looks over at the dog; he looks up at her.

ANNA

Nothing again.

She takes the rat and cuts it up with an old knife, throws a piece to the dog, and bites into her own share.

EXT. GANG TERRITORY / MARKET — DAY

"The Toll"

A square that was once a market and is one again — except that all the traders are fourteen. Stalls made of door panels, tolls at the entrances, rituals nobody explains.

Anna wants through. Two GANG BOYS step into her path, casually, the way a door swings shut.

She gives way to the left — there is a third. To the right: a stall, a wall. It looks like chance and is craft: three steps, and Anna stands in a corner from which the only way on is through the boys.

The dog beside her. Still, for now.

THE LEADER comes without hurry: fifteen, wiry, with the authority of a man who will never become one. He appraises Anna. The bundle. The notebook under her arm.

LEADER

What's that?

ANNA

Paper.

He holds out his hand. Anna gives him the notebook — it is the first time we see it in strange hands, and it is hard to look at.

The leader leafs through it. Crossed-out signs, page after page. He glances at Anna — a look like a shrug: mad, but worthless. He hands it back.

Then he nods to the boys. One reaches for Anna's bundle.

Under Anna's jacket, at the waistband: the old knife. Her hand lies on it without our having seen it go there.

She calculates — faster than you can breathe; we only see it because we know this face by now: three in front of her, one beside, a wall at her back.

The hand comes out from under the jacket empty.

And then there is something YELLOW between Anna and the boys.

The dog. Low, the fur on his back a crest, teeth bared — and out of the thin body comes a sound nobody expected of him. The foremost boy snatches his hand back. The dog snaps — air, close. A warning, not a wound.

The boys give way. All but one.

The leader has stayed where he is. He looks at the dog the way other people look at tools.

LEADER

He hunts.

It is not a question. Anna does not answer. That is answer enough.

LEADER

The dog, then.

EXT. GANG TERRITORY — DAY

"The Price"

Anna looks at the dog.

The dog still stands between her and the boys. Ready. He is waiting for a sign from her — and whatever it says, he will do it.

Anna calculates.

She raises her hand. Flat, calm, downward: stay.

The only command she has ever given him.

The dog stays. A gang boy lays the rope over him, carefully, with respect for the teeth.

Anna does not turn around. She walks through the entrance they now clear for her and grows smaller between the stalls.

EXT. EDGE OF THE TERRITORY — DAY

"The Mended Sole"

At the edge of the square, where the stalls end: a BIG BOY making himself small, bent over a trap — a bracket, a spring, a mechanism he tenses and releases with two fingers, tenses and releases.

He does not look at Anna as she passes.

He looks at her shoes.

JAKOB. But we do not know that yet.

INT. ATTIC — EVENING

Anna takes off her shoes. Stops.

The torn sole of the left one: mended. Properly, with wire and a piece of rubber.

Anna looks at the sole for a long time. She does not know when it happened. Neither do we.

Her gaze goes to the door. To the empty spot where the dog used to lie.

For the first time since the home she looks as if something had escaped her.

Sequence 4 — The Boy (~15 min)

EXT. GANG TERRITORY, WORKSHOP CORNER — DAY

"The Mender"

Anna comes back. This time with a pretext: a rusty padlock without a key, which she lays on the plank in front of Jakob.

He takes it without asking where from, what for. While he works, we see his place in the tribe:

A gang boy brings a cart with a broken axle and leaves it without a word. Another collects the leader's trap, tensioned, oiled, and nods to Jakob.

Jakob is the tolerated one, the one who makes things run. The only one, his whole bearing says, who was taken in here without a beating.

He wears a jacket that almost fits him. Almost — the way things almost fit that you grow into rather than the other way around.

The lock springs open. He hands it to her, with the new key made of wire.

She leaves. He watches her shoes go.

EXT. GANG TERRITORY, WORKSHOP CORNER — DAY

"The Speedometer"

A plank across two barrels, on it tools in an order that resembles no order and is one anyway. Jakob is waiting. In front of him, wrapped in a cloth, an object.

Anna comes. Stops. The distance: two steps, as always.

Jakob unwraps it. A bicycle speedometer. Round, glass, a needle at zero. He hands it over with both hands, solemnly, like a medal.

JAKOB

Fixed.

Anna turns the thing over. No crack, no rust. And no bicycle, anywhere — there are no bicycles left in this city.

ANNA

It doesn't show anything.

JAKOB

(gravely)
Zero. Exactly right.

Anna looks at him for a long time.

Then she stands differently — chin up, a half turn, an appraising look from the corner of the eye: the GANG LEADER, to the millimeter. Soundless. Exact.

Jakob's laughter comes too late. Then it comes entirely — the whole body, a laughter that falls over. He is embarrassed at once, right in the middle of it, and keeps laughing.

Anna does not laugh. She pockets the speedometer. Like a tool.

She leaves. Jakob looks after her. Then he sorts tools that are sorted.

EXT./INT. GANG TERRITORY → LANES → A DOOR — LATE AFTERNOON

"The Half-Done Thing"

The workshop corner. On the plank, half taken apart: the SPYGLASS — Anna's spyglass, at last a real defect. Lenses, rings, the empty tube: parts in a row only Jakob can read.

Jakob works. Anna sits on the second barrel, in a waiting position that has long stopped being one.

Then Jakob looks up — not at Anna: at the light. The stand of the sun above the roofs.

And packs up. Wordless, hurried, with an order that knows no haste and is haste anyway: parts into the cloth, cloth under his arm. The half-done spyglass he takes with him.

No invitation. No look back.

The boy who never talks about himself has a clock in his body.

Anna stays seated for a moment. Then she follows — her property, says her bearing.

Through lanes she does not know. A quarter, narrower, lower; washing lines without washing. Jakob walks fast and naturally, she keeps her distance like an investigator.

Then a door. Jakob goes in without closing it.

Anna stops. At the threshold.

INT. WORKSHOP FLAT — LATE AFTERNOON

"Ruth"

From the door, with Anna: a room that is workshop and home at once. Tools on the wall, tidy. A bed by the window.

In the bed lies a WOMAN. RUTH (50s, built broad and made narrow by the illness; a face that has laughed a lot, and you can still see it).

Jakob already sits at her bed, a bowl of water beside him, washing her arms.

The arms are covered in marks. Many, old and new. Jakob washes them with the casualness of a boy who has never known anything else — skin that needs water, his hand says no more than that.

Anna, in the doorway, sees someone TOUCH marks for the first time in her life.

Ruth turns her head. Sees Anna. Seconds.

RUTH

In or out. But don't stand there in the light — I don't have much of that left.

She steps across the threshold.

Ruth watches Jakob wring out the cloth.

RUTH

He washes me every day. I tell him: boy, save yourself the trouble, the worms will take me dirty too.
(pause)
But no. He wants it done properly.
(to Anna, confiding)
He doesn't get that from me.

Anna laughs. Short, through the nose, against her will — we do not know this laugh yet, it is as new as the room.

Jakob gets up, puts the bowl away and goes outside.

OUTSIDE, at the door: Jakob breathes. Once, twice, three times. Deep. Then he goes back in.

INT. WORKSHOP FLAT — NIGHT

"The Cot"

The spyglass, half reassembled, on the workbench. It has grown late; the kerosene burns low.

Nobody sends Anna away.

She sits in the corner by the door, in her place — without thinking she has chosen the one with two ways out. Ruth sleeps. Jakob tidies tools that are tidy.

In the other corner: a COT. Stretched flat, unused. A bed on which a man should be lying who is not there. Anna sees it. Catalogues it. Does not ask.

LATER: Anna, half asleep, on a blanket on the floor.

Next door, behind the thin wall: sounds. An awl through leather. Thread being pulled through. Even, practiced, half the night.

Anna falls asleep before she can decide what it is.

IN THE MORNING: The cot is as smooth as in the evening. On the workbench lies the spyglass. Finished. Nobody is there who could have been the one.

INT. WORKSHOP FLAT — DAY

"The Laugh"

The flat has grown used to Anna. She sits in her place by the door; Jakob is looking for a tool he tidied away himself.

RUTH

He's been tidying up since he was three. Hasn't found a thing since.

Anna laughs. Through the nose, against her will.

And Ruth goes still.

She looks at Anna — too long, and suddenly quite clear and quite far away at once: the way you look at something that last stood in a room half a lifetime ago.

RUTH

(half aloud, to nobody)
Someone laughed like that once.

ANNA

Who?

RUTH

It's been a long time.

Jakob finds the tool. The moment is over.

Anna registers it. She just does not know where to put it.

EXT. MARKET — DAY

"The Line of Sight"

The gangs' market, everyday life: trading, counting, tolls.

Somewhere in between: two GANG BOYS on the ground, a tangle of sleeves and dust. Then one tears free and runs — something in his fist. What, you cannot see.

The other picks himself up and shouts after him:

GANG BOY

Go to Jahn!

Nobody on the square turns their head. The curse sits.

Anna and Jakob at a stall — she is trading her half pigeon for onions, he is waiting.

At the other end of the square: movement. A MAN (gaunt, long sleeves), drunk — the slow, concentrated walk of a drunk who knows that everyone knows what he is. He crosses the market.

Jakob sees him first. Long before Anna could see him.

And then, casually, as if by chance: Jakob changes sides, takes half a step to the left, turns to Anna, shows her something at the stall — onions, a sieve, whatever.

He places himself exactly in her line of sight. He stays in it, with small corrections, until the man is off the square.

Anna has seen nothing.

EXT. ROOF ABOVE THE LANE — EVENING

"Two Secrets, One Peace"

Two children, a flat roof, remnants of food between them.

Anna's notebook lies beside her — the binding has come loose, the endpaper hangs.

Jakob sees it. Holds out his hand.

Anna looks at the hand. One second. Two.

Then she gives him the notebook — closed.

Jakob sews the binding. Awl, thread, the sounds from that night. He does not open the notebook while he works. Not even halfway. He works on the closed book, more awkwardly than he would have to, and turns the awkwardness into a matter of course.

Anna watches him. Closely. The whole time.

He hands it back. The binding holds.

JAKOB

The thread is better than the notebook.

ANNA

I know.

INT. — NIGHT

"The Night, Second Time"

Again the floorboards. Again the yellow circle.

THE NIGHT, A SECOND TIME. But:

The lamp stands on the table, not on the floor. The curtain hangs at a different window. And the whispering woman's voice says words we know:

MOTHER (O.S.)

(whispering)
... a place, high above everything ...

A KNOCK. The bolt. The loud word that does not come through.

The arm stretches into the light: the gun, the mark — hyper-sharp, exactly as the first time. Only everything else has moved.

Whoever was paying attention now mistrusts their own memory for the first time: that is not how it was.

Or is it?

Anna starts out of sleep. Attic, darkness, her breath.

Her lips begin to count.

Sequence 5 — The City's Lie (~15 min)

INT. CORRIDORS OF THE DYING ROOMS — DAY

"Carrying Water"

A long corridor in a house that was once something else. Doors behind which people are dying. VOLUNTEERS carry water — buckets, jugs, cups.

Anna carries water.

She has a system, and we see it without anyone explaining it: she takes the rooms where washing is done, the corridors where sleeves are rolled up. She holds cups, supports arms, sees wrists. In the evening: draw, cross out.

And casually, in the passing of the days: whoever brings water eats along. A DYING WOMAN wordlessly pushes half a plate toward her; an OLD MAN lays bread on the rim of her bucket without looking at her. The dying share what they no longer need.

INT. A DYING ROOM — DAY

"The Old Woman with the Beat, I"

A bed by the window. THE OLD WOMAN (80s, small, tough). She drinks Anna's water in small sips. Anna lays her notebook on the windowsill. On an old page you can see the words of a headline, "15 Years of Terror," yellowed. The old woman looks at it and taps her fingers on it.

THE OLD WOMAN

Is that from today?

ANNA

What? That — no, probably not.

THE OLD WOMAN

Well? What do you think?

ANNA

Don't know.

The old woman looks at her. Then she laughs — a short, hard laugh that delivers the beat along with it.

THE OLD WOMAN

Terrorists don't build power plants, child. Nothing could ever be fast enough.
(she taps the beat on the edge of the bed)
Faster. Faster. Faster.

She hands Anna back the glass.

THE OLD WOMAN

Jahn never convinced me, back then. But intent?

Anna takes the glass. Registers it. Files it away, in the column for later.

EXT. RUIN BY THE RIVER — EVENING

"JAH ERK"

The works. Huge, burst open, gnawed at by plants — the ruin of a pride.

Above the main gate, letters three meters tall, half fallen:

JAH ERK

What is missing lies below in the rubble and has been burned, built in, vanished, letter by letter.

Anna walks past along the bank of the river in front of it.

EXT. THE FIELDS — NIGHT

"The Harvest"

Beyond the last house: the FIELDS. Man-high stalks as far as the moonlight reaches, a blossoming that looks grey in the dark — and a smell that numbs; you can see it on the faces.

The gang moves out, a dozen children with knives and sacks. Anna goes along; her place is at the back, her share will be small.

LEADER

(half aloud, walking)
Cut, don't tear.

Cutting is done: umbels, in a rhythm the smallest ones master fastest. Pressed into bales, tied.

At the BARRIER — concrete, wire, a dead floodlight — the bales change sides without a word: through a hole. From the other side come CANNED GOODS, crate after crate.

On one of the crates, absurdly clean in all the mud: a LABEL with a curved sign: τ. We know it. From the poster.

Anna cuts, presses, carries. Her share: two cans. One of them she will hide.

EXT. EDGE OF THE FIELDS — NIGHT

"The Flacon"

On the way back, where the field turns to mud: something gleams.

Anna bends down. A FLACON — the glass from the poster, gone dull, the beautiful stopper tarnished. Inside: a remnant, a few drops, darker than water.

She looks around. Nobody. She opens it.

Her knees give way. She sits down in the mud, right in it. Her eyes run over — without anything being sad; that is the unsettling part: her face knows nothing, only the tears run.

She holds the flacon in both hands like a chick.

That is how Jakob finds her. He is trailing the others a little, as always. He stops. Looks at her, in the mud, with the vial.

He does not ask.

He crouches down next to her, within reach, no closer. After a while, dead serious:

JAKOB

If you're going to eat them — the flowers taste of soap.

Anna laughs. And cries. Both at once, and she cannot explain either.

The stopper goes back on the flacon. The flacon disappears into her jacket, where the notebook lives.

NO INSERT on the vial. No memory. Whoever carries the hideout from the first night may remember. Anna cannot.

EXT. ROOF — EVENING

"The First Run"

Over the city comes a sound like a second heart: DRUMS. Not fast. Deep.

Anna is up first — of course she is up first. Jakob follows, grip by grip, grey in the face. He hates heights. He comes anyway. Anna sees it.

From up here, across the street canyon:

The MAIN STREET. Torches, a procession. DRUMMERS at the front, then — two dozen RUNNERS, old men and women, in light clothes. With BARE ARMS.

For the first time in this film, someone displays marks like medals.

Anna, at the edge of the roof. From her jacket: the spyglass.

For the first time through whole lenses — and the first thing she sees sharp is bare arms in torchlight.

The city stands along the edges of the street, dense, still — and sings. Something half-forgotten, more hum than song — drawn out, heavy, in repetitions, like something that must have come into being at work. It does not sound sad. It does not sound glad either. It sounds old.

At the edge of the city, where the street opens into the fields, the runners begin to run.

Really to run. Like children. Like the mad.

The stalks swallow them one after another. The singing stops, not all at once — it frays. Then only the blossoming is there, and the drumming, which slows like a dying heart. Then that stops too.

EXT. BACK ACROSS THE ROOFS — NIGHT

"Only More Honest"

Two children on a ridge, one behind the other, slow. For a long time nothing but their steps.

Then, without occasion:

JAKOB

My father says it's like a holiday. Only more honest.

He says it like a joke you have inherited and do not quite understand.

Anna does not answer.

INT. A DYING ROOM — DAY

"The Old Woman, II: The Wife"

The old woman again. There is less of her.

Anna refills the water. The old woman, casually, from the middle of a thought:

THE OLD WOMAN

Oh, Jahn could talk. Rosy, but — how do you say — not much esprit. His jokes always landed nowhere.

Anna sets down the jug. And asks — a shade too fast:

ANNA

Have you ever seen a man with a mark on his forearm? Here.

The old woman looks at her. Properly, for the first time.

And then Anna does something she has never done: she takes out the notebook. Opens the first page — the soft-worn one we have never seen —

THE OLD WOMAN

Child.
(pause)
Everyone here is marked.

Sequence 6 — The Lookout (~12 min)

EXT. WATER TOWER — DAY

"The Missing Step"

Anna climbs the water tower — routine, grip by grip, the highest point of the quarter. At the top, from her jacket: the spyglass.

She looks out over the city.

Suddenly a HILL, at the far end, behind an old depot. A path turning upward in switchbacks, a railing as a thin line — and at one point, where the path disappears behind the sheds, it breaks off. A gap in the picture.

Like a staircase with one step missing.

Anna looks. Looks away. Looks again.

EXT. PATH TO THE LOOKOUT — DAY

"The Ascent"

The path in switchbacks, the railing: rusty, gone in places.

Anna walks ahead — and her lips are counting. Soundless, out of habit, out of something older than habit. She does not notice it herself.

Jakob is along too, at the back. Pale. Carefully he walks behind her.

EXT. LOOKOUT — DAY

"The Panorama"

At the top.

The wide view: the whole city at once. The ruin with its fallen letters. The fields, a grey sea to the horizon. The barrier: a thin line. And beyond it, incomprehensibly —

world. Simply going on.

Jakob arrives, his eyes opening wide. He tries to breathe the view in. He comes to stand next to Anna. We see both faces.

For the first time Anna stands at a high place and is not alone. Jakob, with his fear of heights, all the more so.

Anna takes his hand.

EXT. PATH DOWN FROM THE LOOKOUT — DAY

"Gone as in Gone"

The way back, in switchbacks. Anna ahead, Jakob behind, one hand on the railing where there is one.

Below, between the roofs, the barrier keeps surfacing and disappearing.

ANNA

Has anyone ever gone through?

JAKOB

(without stopping)
One of the gang — his uncle knew someone who tried.

ANNA

And?

Jakob shrugs. It is not a shrug. It is the entire answer.

JAKOB

Gone.

ANNA

Gone as in through? Or gone as in gone?

JAKOB

Gone as in never seen again.

Anna looks down at the barrier until the roofs swallow it again.

INT. WORKSHOP FLAT — MORNING

"Ruth's Good Day, I: The Condensed Milk"

Ruth's good day. You recognize it at once: she is half sitting up, there is light in the room, and she is talking.

RUTH

Three years now they've been throwing crates out of the sky at us. You know what I've kept, out of that whole sky?
(pause)
The condensed milk.

Jakob grins over his work. Anna, at the table, laughs her nose-laugh.

INT. WORKSHOP FLAT — MIDDAY

"Ruth's Good Day, II: Anton"

The light has wandered. Ruth is lying down again. Her gaze finds Jakob, and something in it has slipped.

RUTH

Anton. The window.

Silence.

JAKOB

(calm, as if it were his name)
I'll do it.

He closes the window. He holds her hand until she sleeps.

Anna watches.

INT. WORKSHOP FLAT — EVENING

"Ruth's Good Day, III: Jakob"

The light is almost gone. Ruth wakes. She looks at Jakob — quite clear, clearer than all day.

RUTH

Jakob.

Once. Right. The way you set a thing in its place.

Jakob holds still, mid-movement.

She says no more. She falls asleep again.

EXT. WORKSHOP YARD — NIGHT

"Lenz Drinks Nothing"

The window of the workshop flat: lit, small. Inside, Jakob sits at the bed.

In the yard stands the man from the market.

LENZ (late 50s, gaunt, long sleeves; hands that were once good at something). Jakob's father. Now he has a name — for us; the city has not told us yet.

Beside him, on the ledge of the wall: a bottle. Home-distilled, full, the cork in.

Lenz stands and drinks nothing.

LATER. The light in the window has grown small.

Inside, Jakob gets up. He stands at the bed for a long time without moving.

Then he opens the window. Wide.

Lenz looks at the open window. He understands it the way you understand something you have waited for all night.

He sits down on the ledge, next to the full bottle. He leaves it closed.

That is how the two of them stay, each on his side of the night: the boy at the open window, the man beside the full bottle. Until it grows light.

A quiet death.

EXT. WASTELAND / BURIAL GROUND — DAY

"The Small Funeral"

A piece of wasteland the city has turned into something like this — rows of flat stones, wooden markers without names.

There are no coffins; wood is warmth. The dead are sewn into cloths, the flat stones from the ruins lie against the dogs. No names stand here.

Ruth's cloth is neatly sewn. The seam is fine. We know whose hand.

Few people. Jakob. Lenz, two steps beside him — two steps that nobody closes. The leader has come, with two of the gang; they stand at the back holding their caps in their hands, and you can see: they have done this too often for their age.

Words are spoken, few. We do not hear them. We do not need to.

Anna stands at the edge, where you stand when you belong to nobody.

EXT. CITY — DAY

"How the Home Fell"

After the grave. They walk

For a long time, nothing.

Then, into the walking, without a run-up:

ANNA

At the home I knew things. Where the sugar was. Who crossed the yard at night.

Jakob does not look at her. That is his way of listening.

ANNA

Someone wanted the sugar. I told him. For half a loaf.

JAKOB

And if they'd caught him?

ANNA

Him. Not me.

Jakob nods.

ANNA

One of the houseparents stole. From the rations, for years. Everyone knew.
(pause)
Once, there hung a slip of paper from over there. With the number.

JAKOB

And?

ANNA

Put it with his lists.

JAKOB

And then?

ANNA

Waited.

Jakob almost stops walking. Then he laughs — the laughter that falls over, in the middle of the street, on the day of a funeral. He is embarrassed at once and keeps laughing.

JAKOB

You waited for someone else to read it out.

ANNA

Was safer.

JAKOB

(still laughing)
And the houseparent?

ANNA

Then the home was over.

She says it the way you close an account. Jakob looks at her from the side — long. For the first time since the grave his face is not grey anymore.

He does not ask why she has told him. It is the longest speech we have ever heard from her.

INT. WORKSHOP FLAT — DAY

"The Prompter"

The room is the same and is not: the bed Ruth lay in is made. The tools on the wall, tidy. The cot empty.

LENZ sits at the table. In front of him stands water he does not touch. He is sober.

ANNA sits on the stool opposite — which she has turned, without thinking, toward the door. Jakob is out getting food.

LENZ

The fields. Four in the morning, in summer. That's when the smell was best — before the sun, while the dew still holds it down. We ran, to be out there by four. Ran. To work.

Anna turns toward him.

Lenz looks at her. For a long time. Almost a smile.

LENZ

I had a friend who couldn't tell a joke. Cleverest man I ever —
(breaks off, starts again)
He could explain a plant to you till you wept, it was that beautiful. But a joke, three sentences — he couldn't carry one across the street in one piece. He always gave the punch line first. Always. Worried you might miss it.

Anna listens the way she listens to everyone. Her eyes go once, casually, to his sleeves. Buttoned.

ANNA

(casually; the casualness of an investigator)
What was his name?

A breath. Lenz's thumb rubs along the edge of the table.

LENZ

My friend.

Anna registers it.

And Lenz talks on as if nothing had been asked:

LENZ (CONT'D)

Ruth always thought him a bit stiff — she liked his girl better. (smiling to himself) The two of them always had plenty to laugh about....
I prompted him. For years. I stood next to him like at the theatre. Sometimes I fed him his own jokes, half aloud, and people laughed, and he took the bow.
(pause)
That was my best one. That nobody noticed who was telling it.

Anna says nothing. But she does not get up.

LENZ (CONT'D)

This one he never got across the street in one piece. Watch.

He straightens up. Barely. But he straightens up.

LENZ (CONT'D)

A man asks the drunkard: why do you drink? Says the drunkard: to forget. Asks the other: forget what?

Lenz lets the pause stand. Exactly as long as it must.

LENZ (CONT'D)

Can't remember.
(beat)
So it's working.

Anna laughs. Once, short, through the nose. Against her will.

Lenz looks up as if a light had come on. He goes to the window, sees nothing.

LENZ

(quietly)
Another?

Sequence 7 — The Child of the Lie (~15 min)

INT. WORKSHOP FLAT — DAY

"The Mayor"

Another day. Jakob is out — a cart, a trap, something.

When Anna knocks, the two cups are already standing on the table.

Lenz has grown thinner; it is going fast now. On his neck, above the collar: a new mark.

He sees Anna's gaze — the gaze that always falls on marks, she cannot switch it off. He points his thumb at it, with the precision of an expert:

LENZ

This one's new. I call him the mayor. Sits at the top and does nothing.

His laugh is dry as kindling.

Anna looks closer. Under the collar, half hidden: another one.

ANNA

(deadpan)
There's another one under your chin.

LENZ

The deputy.

Anna laughs through her nose. Lenz looks at her with that hungry gaze, and for a moment these are two people who are doing well.

The clock on him is running. We can see it now with the naked eye.

EXT. WASTELAND / BURIAL GROUND — DAY

"Anton"

Ruth's stone. Jakob crouches in front of it, straightening what has not slipped: the flat stone, the wooden marker. Work that is none. Anna stands beside him.

For a long time, nothing.

ANNA

Who is Anton?

Jakob moves the stone a finger's width. And back again.

JAKOB

My brother. Never met him.
Died back then, in the night, with the others. He was two. That's all I know.

ANNA

Hm. Weird.

JAKOB

Mama doesn't mean it badly.

Anna looks at him.

Anna sits down on the remnant of wall beside the grave. And pays back:

ANNA

I don't know who mine were. Parents.
(pause)
I have voices. Whispering, nothing more. One word that was loud — once. And a story that keeps coming back and somehow stops.

Jakob does not ask how it goes on. He does not even ask what it is about.

JAKOB

Will you tell it to me sometime?

ANNA

(after a while)
Perhaps.

It is not a no. Both of them know it.

They stay until the sun is off the wasteland.

INT. WORKSHOP FLAT — DAY

"The Chairs"

Another day, the same water he does not drink. The routines grow darker; they slide closer to something.

LENZ

I once set up chairs in a hall. A whole night. Four hundred chairs. For an assembly, in the morning —
(he aligns invisible rows of chairs)
Chairs, child. I hauled chairs for the future. And meanwhile —

Break-off. His hand wipes something from the table that is not there.

But nothing more comes. Instead, out of nowhere:

LENZ

How old are you?

ANNA

Fourteen.

Lenz nods.

Then he gets up, puts away the cup that does not need putting away.

INT. ATTIC — NIGHT

"Sentences Side by Side"

Anna lies awake, eyes open, the skylight a grey rectangle.

And the sentences come. As voices, quiet, out of the dark — in her head, with us as witnesses:

THE OLD WOMAN (O.S.)

... a schoolteacher. She laughed in his speeches ...

MOTHER (O.S.)

... a place, high above everything ...

LENZ (O.S.)

How old are you?

Silence.

Anna sits up.

She looks to the skylight. Sets out.

EXT./INT. THE HOME, ARCHIVE WINDOW — NIGHT

"I Have Tea"

The home at night, from outside: grown darker. Somewhere a little light is burning.

Anna climbs. The archive window — naturally — she knows from before: the latch that does not quite close.

She slips inside —

In the archive room a light is burning.

THE PENCIL sits at the small table between the shelves as if he had been waiting a decade. In front of him: a pot, two cups.

Two.

THE PENCIL

I have tea.

As if that were an explanation.

Anna stands at the window, half in, half out. He did not see her coming — he HOPED for it.

She comes all the way in.

He pours. The pot does not tremble.

THE PENCIL

"Tea is the only drink that makes time stand still."
(pause)
That was in a book. The book has been burned for warmth. The sentence has not.

Anna drinks. It is the best thing anyone has offered her in weeks.

INT. ARCHIVE — NIGHT

"The Little Horse"

Shelves, files, the remains of an administration. Anna stands at the table.

ANNA

My intake. There has to be something. A paper.

The Pencil looks at her. Then he gets up — and fetches no paper.

From the lowest shelf, behind the files: a small bundle of cloth, tied, ten years of dust in its folds.

He lays it in front of her and unwraps it, carefully, with his long grey fingers.

Inside lies a WOODEN LITTLE HORSE.

Small, smooth to the hand, the mane almost loved away — a thing that has spent a thousand hours in a child's hand.

THE PENCIL

This was with you when you came.

Anna does not move.

ANNA

Who brought me?

THE PENCIL

That I don't know. Not anymore, probably. The horse I hid back then, just in case. ...

It fits her hand as if the hand had waited ten years for nothing else. Her thumb finds a spot on the mane by itself.

The notebook in her pocket grows heavy as a millstone. She does not reach for it.

We understand one second before her, and then she understands.

INT. — NIGHT

"The Night, Third Time"

THE NIGHT, A THIRD TIME. The strangest:

The room is almost empty. The furniture wrong, too little of it, in the wrong places. Where the vial stood: nothing.

And the voices are exchanged — the woman's voice, whispering, says the man's words; the man's voice is a sound without words.

In the hand of the child under the table lies, matter-of-factly, as if it had always been there:

the little horse.

(Was it there? Was it ever there?)

A KNOCK. The bolt. The door.

And the father's loud word — ten years long nothing but sound — is for the first time almost language: two syllables.

Two syllables that could be "Lorenz." Or anything else.

Whether memory finally releases the word or is only now building it cannot be decided. We have just seen what it can do with a little horse.

The arm stretches into the light. The gun. The mark. Hyper-sharp — the only thing that always stays the same.

And we no longer believe it a single image.

EXT. ROOF OF THE HOME — NIGHT

"The Overlay"

Anna sits on the ridge of the home, where she sat as a child. Below her the dark house, before her the darker city.

The little horse in her fist. The notebook on her knees.

Anna's hand lies on the first page of the notebook.

She does not open it.

INT. CLASSROOM, THE HOME — NIGHT

"The Fourth Face"

The way down from the roof leads through the house. A corridor, an open door: the classroom. Moonlight on empty benches.

Anna means to pass. And goes in.

On the wall the chart still hangs. The works, proud, seen from below. In front of it, small, the four people with the flags.

Anna has looked at this picture for a thousand hours and never seen it.

Three faces are scratched out, the grey shiny with years. The fourth nobody has touched: a young woman, laughing, a child on her arm.

Anna knows the face. She last saw it when there was less of it left.

Ruth.

And next to her, one of the scratched-out ones: the face is gone, the bearing has stayed. She stands turned a little, and one hand lies on the dress, low, the way hands lie when they are guarding something.

Anna stands before the picture for a long time and strokes the woman's belly.

EXT. DISTILLERY, WALL — DAY

"The Chalk List"

The old distillery: a long brick building, dead chimneys, the smell of back then long gone from the walls.

On the yard wall: NAMES. Written in chalk, one below the other, some weathered, some fresh. A list nobody administers and everybody respects.

Whoever stands here will be washed, and runs.

JAKOB stands in front of the wall. Alone.

His gaze goes down the names — and catches.

There, in neat, old-fashioned handwriting, with the full name, first and last, fresh:

LORENZ KESSLER

Jakob stands very still.

EXT. OUTSIDE THE WORKSHOP — DAY

"The Request"

Anna comes — and Jakob is already standing there, outside the door, as if he had not been able to go in.

JAKOB

He put himself on it. Properly. Full name.

Anna understands at once what is meant. The whole city would understand.

ANNA

(automatic)
What's his name?

JAKOB

Lorenz.

Silence.

Something sounds in Anna — two syllables, deep under ten years. Or she is building it in this moment. It cannot be decided, not for her, not for us.

Her face does nothing. That is work, and you can see the work.

Then, for the first time since we have known him, Jakob wants something:

JAKOB

You're quick. You think of things.
(pause)
Help me get him down off there.

Anna — who has never refused him a request, because he never had one — says nothing.

One second. Two. One second too long.

Jakob looks at her. Sees something he cannot name.

JAKOB

You look at him like your grown-ups.
(quieter)
Like one out of your notebook.

ANNA

Get him down. How. Tell me how.

JAKOB

You think of things everywhere else. With the sugar. With the lists. With your dog you thought of something too —

ANNA

The dog was a toll.

JAKOB

The dog was yours. You didn't even wait for them to take hold of him. You nodded and you walked.

ANNA

And you climbed after me onto every roof, grey as ash, and never said a word. You hid your father from me. I see everything.
(pause)
You take a look at him. He doesn't have long — wall or no wall.
(calm; the calm is the worst of it)
And if that is his last wish, then let him go.

JAKOB

(the quietest sentence of the fight)
Yes. You see everything. And say nothing. You sit with him, you listen to him, for weeks — and the one time I need something, you calculate.

ANNA

I don't calculate —

JAKOB

I watched. One second. You calculated like at the toll.

Anna is silent.

JAKOB

People who only ever take — those I know. The whole city takes. I thought —

He breaks off. He does not say what he thought.

Jakob goes. Anna stays standing and looks after him.

EXT. OUTSIDE THE WORKSHOP — NIGHT

"The Spyglass at the Door"

Anna does not come to knock.

Through the window, out of the dark of the alley: inside, the small lamp is burning. Jakob sits by the cot — on which the father now lies, sleeping; for the first time we see Lenz on this bed. Jakob simply sits there. Keeping watch.

Then she steps to the door. From her jacket: the spyglass.

She sets it on the threshold. Centered. Properly.

And goes.

Sequence 8 — The Run (~12 min)

EXT. DISTILLERY COURTYARD — MORNING

"The Washing" · "The Writing on the Arms" · "The Perhaps" · "The Little Horse on the Table"

Cold in which you can see breath. Kettles of warm water, carried by VOLUNTEERS — nobody assigns them, they come of their own accord. The dying arrange it themselves.

Steam stands over the courtyard.

The RUNNERS, a dozen, lay down what they have carried: jackets, shirts, things. A TABLE for the things. Nobody guards it.

The volunteers wash those who run today. Arms, face, neck — slow, thorough, without ceremony. It looks like what it is: people washing people.

And with the laying down and the washing there are — arms. Bare arms, everywhere in the courtyard, marks in every script. What the city has shrouded since we have known it is simply here. Nobody points at it. It is the only place where it means nothing.

On the other side of the courtyard: LENZ, with bare arms.

Marks, many, old and new — a whole script. We see him without sleeves for the first time, and it is as if we were seeing a man naked for the first time.

Before him JAKOB. The camera's gaze catches his shoes.

Freshly mended. Seams as fine as only one man makes them. Found in the morning by his bed — we know now what the father's last night was spent on.

Jakob says nothing. Lenz says nothing.

Then the father's hands rise. Halfway. At half height they stop and fall back down.

Between the two stands fourteen years of air.

ANNA stands at the edge.

Anna's gaze does what her gaze always does. It falls on the arms.

The steam opens. Two seconds, three: on the inner forearm, among many — a mark in the shape of the horse's head.

Perhaps. Perhaps not. The steam moves back in between.

NO INSERT. NO CLOSENESS. NO MUSIC. We are too far away to swear to it, and we stay too far away.

Anna closes her eyes.

She opens them again. Now she looks at his face. Lenz returns the gaze. Briefly, then back to Jakob.

Again we are on Anna.

Then she walks to the table with the laid-down things. Lays, between the jackets and shirts, the wooden little horse. She simply does it.

The camera stays with her and with the table until she turns away.

EXT. MAIN STREET — DAY

"The Procession"

The DRUMS set in, deep, like a second heart.

The procession: the drummers at the front, then the runners, in light clothes, with bare arms. Lenz among them — more upright than we have ever seen him, and not yet upright.

The city stands along the edges, dense. And sings the half-forgotten thing. Closer now, close enough for words — a work song, drawn out and heavy: one line that repeats as if it had to keep a back straight, and a third that answers.

Early at four, before the sun stands high,

early at four, before the sun stands high,

I go into the dew, before the dew goes dry.

Old voices carry the words; the cohort knows them only half and hums the gaps. It is the harvest song. The city sings after its runners what it sang all its life on the way to work.

JAKOB runs the first stretch along the edge, level with his father.

The corner where the street opens into the fields. There the living stop. That is the rule nobody explains.

Jakob stops. The procession runs on. From behind, Anna comes running up.

EXT. THE FIELDS — DAY

"The Supernova"

The street becomes a field path, the path becomes a trace, the stalks stand man-high and blossom.

At the edge of the fields the runners begin to run.

Really to run. Like children. Like the mad.

And Lenz runs.

In the running something straightens up in him that we have taken for dead the whole film: the shoulders come back. The stride takes hold. The field worker finds a ground he knows — this ground, there is no other he knows like this.

The stalks close over the first one. Over the second.

Just before the blossoming takes him, Lenz turns his face into the smell — the best nose in the city, in the smell of its life —

and LAUGHS.

It is the only time we see him laugh.

Then there is only the field.

The drumming slows. Stops.

EXT. CITY / PATH TO THE HILL — DAY

"The Stillness After"

The city is very still. It has never been this still — and it was always still.

Anna and Jakob walk side by side. Without touching. Without talking.

Around Jakob's neck, on a new strap, fine seams: the spyglass.

They walk the path up the hill, in switchbacks, along the railing.

EXT. LOOKOUT — DAWN

"The Story, Aloud"

The city below is grey and still and complete. The thin line of the barrier. Beyond it: world, simply going on.

ANNA and JAKOB sit side by side at the railing. Not touching. The distance: a hand's width.

For a long time, nothing. Then:

ANNA

Mama always talked about us sitting above the city one day. I think it's here.

Jakob does not look at her. That is his way of listening.

Anna looks at the city. Then she recites it — not the way you tell, the way you recite: a text that has been by heart for ten years.

ANNA

High above everything there is a place. You take the path that turns, always by the railing, and you count the steps. And at the top the whole city lies at your feet, like an open book. And in the evening comes the —

She holds.

Not like someone thinking. Like someone walking a staircase in the dark, one they know — and the step is not there.

Jakob does not ask.

JAKOB

(quietly)
Go on.

ANNA

— and it lays itself over everything, and everything that is broken looks whole from up there. At this place we promised you your life. And whoever has been up there knows: behind the city the world does not end. When you are big, we will go up, we three. And you will count the steps.

Pause.

Jakob nods. Looks at the city, the barrier, the thin line.

JAKOB

Mine kept nothing from the Drops but the condensed milk.

Anna laughs. Quietly, through the nose. She does not lean against him. She only sits a little less alone.

Then Jakob lays his hand on the railing, next to hers.

EXT. ROAD TO THE BARRIER — DAY

"The Sign"

A road between fields, toward the thin line on the horizon.

At the roadside, crooked in the earth: an official SIGN from before. The lettering almost gone, eaten by weather:

RESTRICTED AREA

NO ENTRY

Nobody looks at it.

Two children walk beneath it, each with a bundle, toward the horizon behind which the world simply goes on.

EXT. THE GANG'S WALL — DAY

"The Dog"

On a wall, far behind them, at the edge of the territory:

the yellow DOG.

He sits and watches them go, the two shrinking figures between the fields.

He does not run after them.

Whether they get through, we do not see. The hope of a way out weighs more.

BLACK.

THE END.
